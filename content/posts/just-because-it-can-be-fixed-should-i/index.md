---
title: "Just Because It Can Be Fixed, Should I?"
date: 2026-11-28T10:30:00+11:00
---
I was about 22.

Full of enthusiasm.

Full of ideas.

And absolutely convinced I could improve just about any process someone put in front of me.

Then I met an Excel ecosystem.

VLOOKUPs.

Pivot tables.

Formulas referencing other formulas.

Multiple interconnected workbooks that looked like they'd developed a life of their own.

**OH, I CAN FIX THIS.**

🤣

I was working with a bookkeeping client, and one of their employees had built an elaborate Excel system to manage timesheets and prepare information for payroll.

It worked.

But it was complicated.

And looking at it, I could see a couple of alternatives.

I could simplify the existing Excel ecosystem.

Or we could go further.

Introduce electronic timesheets that fed directly into the payroll system.

Less manual handling.

Fewer moving parts.

A process that would potentially be easier to maintain.

**EXCELLENT. LET'S FIX EVERYTHING.**

😂

Except...

The employee who managed the process liked her system.

She understood it.

She was comfortable with it.

And the business valued her and was happy with how things were working.

My alternatives might have been technically simpler.

But implementing either one would have meant changing how people worked.

New processes.

New habits.

Training.

Getting people comfortable with something unfamiliar.

And nobody was particularly interested in doing all that at the time.

Which was fair enough.

I was there to help with bookkeeping.

Not to redesign their entire payroll process.

And while 22-year-old me was generally quite enthusiastic about fixing everything within a five-kilometre radius...

Even I could see that this probably wasn't the moment.

🤣

So I left it alone.

I worked with the existing system.

And something about that decision stuck with me.

Because I'd been looking at the spreadsheets as a technical problem.

But was it actually a problem that needed solving?

The process worked.

The people responsible for it were comfortable with it.

The business wasn't asking for a replacement.

And I hadn't established that the disruption of changing it would be worth the benefit.

**WAS THE PROBLEM REALLY THE SPREADSHEETS?**

**OR WAS I ABOUT TO CREATE A NEW PROBLEM BY TRYING TO FIX THEM?**

That's an interesting distinction.

A technically simpler solution isn't necessarily a better outcome for the people expected to use it.

And recognising an alternative doesn't automatically mean you should implement it.

I still love finding ways to improve things.

Give me an unnecessarily complicated process, and my brain will immediately start looking for ways to simplify it.

But these days, I try to ask myself another question before I get too excited.

Not just:

**CAN I FIX THIS?**

But:

**SHOULD I?**

Because sometimes the best contribution isn't introducing a new system.

It's recognising when leaving a working one alone is the better decision.

And occasionally telling your inner process-improvement enthusiast to sit down.

**APPARENTLY, NOT EVERY SPREADSHEET NEEDS TO BE RESCUED.**

🤣
