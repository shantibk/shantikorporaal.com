### I Recruited an AI Homer Coach. Things Got Out of Hand.

I'd tried reading *The Iliad* and *The Odyssey* before.

More than once.

They'd been on my reading list for years. Important books. Classic literature. The sort of thing I felt I really should have read by now.

There was just one problem.

**I HAD ABSOLUTELY NO IDEA WHAT WAS GOING ON.**

🤣

I'd even tried watching the movie *Troy* when I was younger.

Didn't get through that either.

Too many unfamiliar characters, relationships, and stories I had no frame of reference for.

But with a new movie on the horizon, I thought it was time to try again.

I was already doing all sorts of things with ChatGPT.

**WELL, WHY NOT THIS?**

I recruited myself a Homer coach.

The instructions were simple.

Help me understand the books. Research the context. Fact-check my interpretations.

And please, **CORRECT ME WHEN I'M WRONG.**

I didn't want an AI enthusiastically agreeing with me while I built an entirely incorrect understanding of ancient Greek literature.

🤣

So I listened to the audiobook, took heaps of notes to help me concentrate, and fed those notes and audio transcripts into ChatGPT.

Then came the questions.

Lots of questions.

And slowly, things started clicking.

The gods were particularly confusing. I'd grown up with a very different religious frame of reference, and these gods were constantly interfering, arguing, and taking sides.

Then I found a mental model that worked.

**THE GODS WERE THE GOVERNANCE BOARD.**

🤣

Suddenly, their competing interests and questionable interventions made sense.

Agamemnon was the executive sponsor who kept threatening to cancel the project.

Achilles was the talented specialist who'd had an enormous falling-out with management.

And the Trojan War?

**THIS WAS AN ENTERPRISE TRANSFORMATION PROGRAM.**

😂

I hadn't planned a LinkedIn series.

But these ridiculous parallels kept appearing, and I needed somewhere to put them.

Somehow, that became 18 articles about the world's messiest project.

And somewhere along the way, I fell in love with the characters.

Hector became my get-off-my-lawn guy.

Achilles became the brutish teenager who wouldn't do what anyone told him.

Nestor became my **"BACK IN MY DAY..."** guy.

Ask him for advice, and you're getting a historical monologue whether you wanted one or not.

**THIS COULD HAVE BEEN A TWO-MINUTE CONVERSATION, NESTOR.**

🤣

And Agamemnon?

Initially, I found him arrogant and thoroughly frustrating.

But then I saw him discouraged, fighting on the battlefield, listening to advisers and attempting to reconcile with Achilles.

He was becoming harder to fit into the box I'd originally put him in.

And that happened with other characters, too.

ChatGPT would challenge my interpretations, reminding me of another scene or another side of someone I'd overlooked.

Eventually, I noticed something about myself.

I make quick initial judgments because my brain likes having somewhere to file new information.

**RIGHT. YOU GO IN THIS BOX.**

🤣

I'm happy to rearrange things as I learn more.

But Homer challenged me to do something different.

**JUDGE LESS. NOTICE MORE.**

I didn't need to decide who was good or bad.

I could let the characters be complicated, contradictory, and human.

One of my favourite moments was Hector saying goodbye to his wife and son.

Such a beautiful family moment amid all that chaos.

I mentioned it to ChatGPT, only to discover it's one of the most celebrated scenes in the poem, inspiring artworks across centuries.

**OH. SO EVERYONE ELSE FELL IN LOVE WITH THIS SCENE TOO.**

🤣

And then there was the wall.

The Greeks built an entire defensive wall, complete with gates and a ditch.

I wanted construction details. Design decisions. Engineering specifications.

Homer?

Not particularly interested.

But Achilles needed new armour?

**SUDDENLY WE HAVE AN EXTENSIVE DESIGN PRESENTATION.**

😂

By the end of *The Iliad*, I was approaching the story differently.

The poem doesn't end with Troy falling or the war ending.

It ends with Hector's funeral.

I found myself wondering whether Homer was saying something about the cost of war, and whether anybody really wins.

I don't know if that's what he intended.

And I was comfortable not knowing.

I'd started enjoying the questions as much as the answers.

That's what made having an AI coach so valuable.

It wasn't doing the reading for me. It was helping me question my understanding, challenge my assumptions, and build the context I was missing.

I wanted to read Homer.

I ended up learning something about how I learn.

And perhaps a little about how I judge people, too.

Eighteen articles later, I've fallen in love with the story, the characters and one spectacularly dysfunctional enterprise transformation program.

Next up?

*The Odyssey.*

The war is over.

The project is complete.

**SURELY GETTING EVERYONE HOME WILL BE STRAIGHTFORWARD.**

🤣
